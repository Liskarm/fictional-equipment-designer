# Visual Language Reference

Use this reference to convert function, faction, era, and production method into a readable equipment design and concept-art brief.

Use the visual thesis, design layers, functional storytelling, concept-art contract, and image review for a simple brief. Add the production/art-bible controls only when the equipment will become a shared, repeated, platform-bound, or approval-gated asset.

## Visual thesis

Write three short statements:

1. **Recognition:** “At a glance, this is a [role/category] because …”
2. **Identity:** “It belongs to [faction/manufacturer/character] because …”
3. **Difference:** “It must not be confused with [neighboring design] because …”

If the function and identity rely entirely on logos or exposition, strengthen silhouette and construction language.

## Design layers

### Silhouette and proportion

- Overall length, height, width, visual mass, and center of gravity at a qualitative level
- Dominant mass, secondary mass, negative space, and directionality
- Operator-to-item scale and carry or mounting posture
- Recognition at the actual camera distance or illustration size
- Asymmetry, folding, articulation, or ritual display only when functionally motivated

### Shape grammar

Choose two or three recurring primitives and a rule for how they meet: monolithic slabs, swept wedges, nested cylinders, exposed frames, ribbed organic forms, textile-wrapped bundles, carved planes, or modular blocks. Define one forbidden shape habit to protect identity.

### Material hierarchy

Assign materials by function and status:

- structural body;
- protected or disposable shell;
- grip and operator-contact surfaces;
- energy, magic, biological, optical, or sensing elements;
- heat-affected, contaminated, sacred, or hazardous zones;
- repairs, substitutions, and field wear.

Use material transitions to explain assembly and maintenance. Do not add random surface complexity.

### Color and markings

Separate base material, faction color, functional color, state light or magical emission, hazard marking, and personal modification. Define how colors change in inactive, ready, active, damaged, contaminated, or exhausted states.

### Detail density

Concentrate detail around interaction, maintenance, sensing, articulation, and signature technology. Preserve calm areas so important detail reads. Match detail scale to platform, camera, and production capacity.

## Faction and lifecycle expression

Derive visual identity from the faction's values and capabilities:

| Driver | Visual evidence |
|---|---|
| Industrial capacity | repeatable modules, handmade variation, scarce finishing, repair culture |
| Doctrine | protection, mobility, intimidation, concealment as fiction, ceremony, expendability |
| Social order | rank markings, personalization rights, sacred restrictions, ownership records |
| Technology culture | exposed proof of function, sealed mystery, biomorphic growth, ritual inscription |
| Supply history | reused parts, incompatible generations, captured components, local substitutes |

Show age through design lineage, not only scratches. A later model should inherit recognizable DNA while revealing changed doctrine or manufacturing.

## Functional storytelling

Visually answer:

- Where does the operator hold, wear, mount, open, feed, charge, or inspect it?
- Which direction is “forward,” active, hazardous, or fragile?
- How does the item communicate safe, ready, active, empty, overheated, cursed, or damaged state?
- Which subsystem is deliberately protected, exposed, replaceable, ceremonial, or disposable?
- What does field repair look like?
- Which marks reveal unit, owner, manufacturer, era, legal status, or previous conflict?

The answers need not be realistic engineering, but they must agree with the fictional mechanism and operator loop.

## Readability and production constraints

Record:

- target medium and camera distance;
- value and color separation from operator and environment;
- animation or transformation states;
- left/right or front/back ambiguity risks;
- effects budget and whether glow, particles, transparency, or complex motion are essential;
- modeling, costume, illustration, or prop constraints;
- repeated asset or variant production rules;
- do/don't examples described in words when images are unavailable.

For a production asset, also record:

- asset class and reuse tier: hero, standard, background, modular kit, cosmetic variant, cinematic-only, illustration-only, or physical prop;
- target platform, engine or renderer, expected camera distance, and accessibility needs;
- schedule, staffing, memory, geometry, texture, material, rig, animation, effects, draw-call, fabrication, or page-space budgets that actually constrain the asset;
- runtime conditions such as damage states, attachment swaps, team colors, skinning, transformation, lighting environments, and level-of-detail behavior;
- which constraints are confirmed, provisional, or owned by another discipline.

Do not invent numeric budgets. Request them from the relevant owner or use explicitly labeled qualitative limits until the project supplies values.

For cosplay concepts, keep parts clearly nonfunctional and venue-safe. Avoid hidden functional mechanisms or realistic construction detail.

## Project style and reference control

For a shared production or art-bible asset, define two to four project style pillars that the equipment must express. Phrase each as an observable decision rule, such as “maintenance seams stay readable at gameplay distance,” rather than a mood adjective alone. Pair the pillars with project-level exclusions and equipment-specific exclusions so teams know both the intended family resemblance and the forbidden shortcuts.

Build a compact reference set with captions that state what to borrow and what to reject:

| Reference | Type | Use for | Do not copy / reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive | project canon, approved asset, material, shape, wear, or motion example | one specific pillar or production solution | unrelated motifs, unsupported functions, or accidental details |
| Negative | known drift, near-miss, competitor, generic output, or rejected internal example | one specific boundary or failure to avoid | treat as a style target or reproduce protected identity |

Use both positive and negative references. A “don't” example must name the failure it demonstrates; a “do” example must name the transferable rule. Do not use reference volume as a substitute for decisions.

Separate **lighting mood** from **readability lighting**:

- Lighting mood defines the narrative atmosphere, time, emotional temperature, and faction presentation.
- Readability lighting and background define neutral or alternate conditions that reveal silhouette, materials, contact points, state cues, and transparent or emissive elements without hiding defects.

Request both when a dramatic hero image alone would make the equipment difficult to evaluate.

## Concept-art brief contract

Include:

1. subject and role;
2. operator, faction, era, and environment;
3. silhouette and proportions;
4. visible fictional mechanisms and interaction points;
5. shape grammar and material hierarchy;
6. palette, markings, wear, and state cues;
7. scale reference;
8. required views: hero view, orthographic-like views, use pose, state or variant callouts as needed;
9. lighting mood for narrative presentation;
10. separate readability lighting and background for evaluation;
11. project style pillars, positive/negative references, and explicit exclusions when the asset must align with an art bible;
12. asset class, platform, budget, and runtime constraints when the design enters production.

Do not request a real-world build diagram. A cutaway, if narratively useful, should depict invented subsystem blocks without actionable construction detail.

## Production ownership and handoff

For an approval-gated or cross-discipline asset, record:

- the visual owner responsible for the current brief and the reviewer or canon/design authority who may approve it;
- approval state: draft, discipline review, revision requested, approved for production, or superseded;
- the approval gate and evidence required to pass it;
- validation checkpoints appropriate to the asset, such as silhouette thumbnail, graybox, material test, rig or articulation test, in-engine gameplay-distance review, effects/readability pass, fabrication mock-up, or final canon audit;
- handoff recipients and decisions needed by concept art, modeling, surfacing, rigging, animation, VFX, technical art, UI/iconography, audio, level design, narrative/canon, marketing/illustration, or fabrication—include only disciplines the equipment actually affects.

At handoff, distinguish approved invariants from adjustable implementation details. Name the source of truth and version or date so downstream teams do not treat an old image as current approval.

## Image review and drift control

After an image is generated, check:

- Does the silhouette match the brief before zooming in?
- Are operator contact points and scale coherent?
- Are active and inactive states legible?
- Are faction cues structural rather than logo-only?
- Did the generator add unsupported functions, text, or extra components?
- Do multiple views depict the same object?
- Does the image contradict mechanism, balance, canon, or safety constraints?
- Does it satisfy the relevant project pillars, exclusions, production limits, and approval gate?

Treat mismatches as design changes requiring approval, not silent improvements.

Revalidate after any change that affects silhouette, operator interaction, signature mechanism, state readability, faction identity, canon status, asset class, platform, budget, runtime behavior, or an approved dependency. Record what changed, which checkpoints must repeat, who approved the deviation, and which downstream disciplines need a refreshed handoff. Minor polish that preserves approved invariants may remain within the current gate; do not let accumulated minor changes create unreviewed drift.
