# Mechanism and Lifecycle Reference

Use this reference to turn a fictional effect into a constrained, operable, and supportable piece of equipment. Maintain fictional abstraction; the goal is internal causality, not real-world construction.

## Capability chain

For every signature capability, fill this chain:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Intended effect | What changes for the operator, target, or environment? |
| Enabler | Which setting rule, technology, organism, ritual, or material makes it possible? |
| Input | What is consumed: charge, ammunition, heat capacity, time, attention, memory, life, money, or permission? |
| Conversion | In broad fictional terms, how does the input become the effect? |
| Control | How does the operator start, shape, limit, or stop it? |
| Feedback | How does the operator know state, readiness, and error? |
| Loss or waste | What heat, noise, residue, corruption, fatigue, signature, or social cost appears? |
| Limitation | What prevents universal or continuous use? |
| Failure | What happens when a component, ritual, user, or supply chain fails? |
| Counter | How can an opponent, environment, institution, or rival technology reduce its value? |

If any major capability lacks one of these links, mark it as an unresolved design debt.

## Constraint ledger

Choose constraints appropriate to the setting rather than forcing modern engineering onto magic or speculative science.

### Material and spatial constraints

- Apparent mass, bulk, balance, reach, storage, and transport
- Operator anatomy, posture, contact points, armor, clothing, mounts, and vehicle interfaces
- Vulnerable or protected subsystems
- Access needed for inspection, replacement, ritual, feeding, cleaning, or calibration
- Environmental limits: dust, water, cold, heat, vacuum, corrosion, contamination, signal denial, or anti-magic fields

### Resource constraints

- Capacity and consumption pattern
- Peak use versus sustained use
- Reload, recharge, recovery, cooling, healing, or ritual reset
- Shelf life, leakage, decay, instability, contamination, or theft
- Standardized versus proprietary supply
- Who can make, move, authorize, and service the resource

### Information and control constraints

- Training and cognitive load
- Identification, targeting, authentication, and permissions
- Latency, uncertainty, interference, deception, and friendly-fire risk
- Manual fallback when automation, magic, or networking fails
- Observable tells available to the user and to opponents

### Supernatural and biological constraints

- Eligibility: bloodline, contract, affinity, implant, infection, belief, or sacrifice
- Cost location: user, target, environment, patron, future self, or community
- Conservation rule: what cannot be created without exchange
- Contamination, mutation, possession, memory change, or moral injury
- Ritual timing, emotional state, taboo, jurisdiction, or celestial conditions

## Operator loop

Describe the complete loop, not only the spectacular moment:

1. **Acquire and transport:** ownership, security, weight, and team burden.
2. **Prepare:** inspect, assemble, attune, unlock, load, charge, authorize, or summon.
3. **Orient:** identify state, select mode, communicate intent, and avoid friendly harm.
4. **Act:** commit, aim or guide, receive feedback, and manage fictional physical or magical consequences.
5. **Interrupt:** cancel safely, recover from surprise, or transition to a backup action.
6. **Reset:** reload, cool, recharge, heal, cleanse, or renegotiate a contract.
7. **Malfunction:** detect, isolate, abandon, or perform a setting-appropriate safe recovery.
8. **Maintain:** clean, repair, feed, update, bless, calibrate, or document.

Use this loop to reveal character and doctrine. Elite, improvised, ceremonial, civilian, and mass-issued equipment should feel different to operate.

## Failure architecture

Use at least one failure from two different categories:

| Category | Examples at fictional abstraction |
|---|---|
| Resource | depleted, contaminated, incompatible, interrupted supply |
| Thermal or metabolic | overheating, exhaustion, recovery debt, tissue stress |
| Control | false input, lockout, desynchronization, corrupted ritual |
| Structural | wear, fracture, seal loss, exposed focus or conduit |
| Environmental | weather, terrain, interference, hostile field |
| Human | training error, panic, divided attention, mistrust |
| Institutional | authorization revoked, maintenance unavailable, doctrine mismatch |
| Ethical or supernatural | unwilling cost, broken vow, patron refusal, unacceptable collateral effect |

Failures should generate decisions, not arbitrary punishment. Define warning signs and a recovery path when the fiction allows one.

## Production and support

Answer these at setting scale:

- Who designed it, who owns the knowledge, and who actually manufactures or creates it?
- Which step is scarce: raw material, craft knowledge, labor, ritual authority, quality control, or transport?
- What distinguishes prototype, elite, mass-production, export, civilian, ceremonial, and illicit versions?
- Which parts, consumables, training, or infrastructure are standardized?
- What maintenance interval and skill level are implied?
- How do field units improvise when official support fails?
- What evidence of manufacturing method appears in seams, fasteners, surface finish, inscriptions, modularity, or repair marks?
- Why is it still in service, and what would cause replacement or retirement?

## Variant discipline

Create a variant only when the operator, mission, environment, legal regime, price tier, historical generation, compatibility standard, or controlling faction changes.

For each variant record: unchanged design DNA, changed subsystem, gained capability, lost capability, new support burden, visual differentiator, and canon status. Avoid pure upgrades unless scarcity, authority, reliability, or narrative cost keeps the base model relevant.

## Precision policy

Use exact numbers only when they already exist in project rules or are needed as normalized game variables. Otherwise prefer ranges, ordinal scales, or comparisons such as “shorter endurance than the standard model.” Do not invent real-world ballistic, explosive, chemical, or manufacturing specifications.
